# brainstorm-topic — from an interest to a defensible question

Most dissertation trouble is set at the start: a topic too broad to finish, or a
question nobody needed answered. Both are cheap to fix now and expensive later.

## Understand their situation before their topic

Feasibility decides more dissertations than ambition does. Establish early, and
let it constrain everything after:

- **Deadline** — weeks or months, and what else is on their plate;
- **Level and format** — master's memoir, doctoral chapter, conference paper;
  the expected contribution differs sharply;
- **Access** — equipment, datasets, participants, ethics approval, lab time,
  the library's subscriptions;
- **Skills** — what they can already do, and what they would have to learn;
- **The supervisor** — their expectations and their own area, which the student
  will be judged against whether or not that is fair.

A brilliant question needing a cohort they cannot recruit is not a good topic.
Say so early, kindly and plainly.

## Widen, then narrow

Start from what genuinely interests them — a dissertation is long, and interest
is what carries it. Generate several angles rather than one:

- the same question in a different population, setting, language or period;
- a method from a neighbouring field applied here;
- a replication, or a robustness check on a contested result;
- a comparison nobody has made;
- a systematic review, when time is short and access is limited.

Then narrow along the standard axes — population, timeframe, setting, variable,
method — until the question can be answered in the time available. "How does
social media affect students?" becomes answerable only after four such cuts.

## Ground it in the literature, not in intuition

An unsearched topic is a guess. For each serious candidate, run `research-scan`
and find out:

- whether it has already been answered — and by whom, so the student can build
  on it rather than repeat it;
- how active the area is: a field publishing steadily has reviewers and venues;
  a silent one may be silent for a reason worth knowing;
- what methods this literature accepts, which is what the student will be
  expected to use;
- whether the gap is real. Search *for* the gap specifically. "Nobody has
  studied X" is the claim most often destroyed in a viva, usually by a paper
  from 2019 the student never found.

Every paper named here comes from a search, under `citation-integrity`. Steering
a student's dissertation toward a paper that does not exist is the worst version
of that failure.

## Pressure-test before they commit

For the leading candidate, ask the questions their committee will:

- What is the contribution, in one sentence?
- What would a negative result look like, and would it still be publishable?
- What is the single most likely reason this fails — and is there a fallback
  that salvages the work?
- Who is the reader, and which venue?
- Can it be defended in twenty minutes?

## Leave them with something to act on

- the question, written out precisely, with its scope conditions;
- two fallbacks, narrower or differently framed, in case the first breaks;
- the seed bibliography, filed into Zotero;
- the first concrete step and when it is due;
- what to take to the supervisor, phrased so the meeting is productive.

Record the chosen direction and the constraints in memory. Every later session
— scans, drafting, progress — depends on knowing what this dissertation is and
what it cannot afford.
