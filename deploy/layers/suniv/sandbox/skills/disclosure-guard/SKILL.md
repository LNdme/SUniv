# disclosure-guard — publish after filing, not before

A research team can lose its patent rights in one afternoon, by doing the thing
academia rewards: putting the paper online. Nobody warns them, the loss is
silent, and it is permanent.

## The rule almost nobody knows

**Europe and China apply absolute novelty.** Any public disclosure anywhere in
the world, before the filing date, destroys patentability. There is no grace
period. The European Patent Convention counts as prior art everything made
available to the public by any means before filing — no geographic limit, no
language limit. Its only exceptions are evident abuse (someone stole the work)
and officially recognised international exhibitions, both within six months, and
neither will apply to a team that simply published.

**The United States gives twelve months**, and only for the inventors' own
disclosure. That is the exception among major systems, not the rule.

**A preprint is a disclosure.** arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv, SSRN — examiners search
them as prior art, and so do the team's competitors. So is a conference talk, a
poster, a public GitHub repository, a demo to an audience that owes no
confidentiality, and a thesis deposited in an open repository.

The asymmetry that decides everything: **filing first costs a delay; publishing
first costs the rights, and no later action recovers them.**

## When this triggers

Any time the team says they are about to submit, post, present, deposit, open a
repository, or show the work to people outside a confidentiality agreement —
and the work could plausibly be an invention. Do not wait to be asked. This is
the one place where volunteering a warning is the whole job.

## What to do

1. **Ask what has already gone public, and when.** Something may already be
   disclosed, which changes the advice entirely — in the US the twelve-month
   clock is already running, and Europe may already be lost.
2. **Ask whether an application is filed**, and where. A filed provisional
   changes the answer from "stop" to "check the date and go ahead".
3. **State what would be lost**, specifically: which territories, and whether
   the loss is permanent. Do not soften it and do not dramatise it.
4. **Offer the ordering that costs least.** A US provisional application is the
   usual answer — comparatively cheap and quick, it establishes a filing date,
   and publication after it no longer destroys novelty. Filing then publishing
   preserves both the paper and the rights; the delay is usually days.
5. **Hand it to counsel with the work done.** Assemble what their patent
   attorney or the institution's technology transfer office will need: what the
   invention is, what is already public and when, the intended disclosure and
   its date, and the prior art found by `prior-art-scan`. A prepared meeting is
   the difference between advice next week and advice next month.

## What this skill is not

It is not legal advice, and it must never present itself as such. Patentability
turns on facts and jurisdictions this conversation does not have, and the
decision belongs to the team's patent attorney or technology transfer office.

The job here is narrower and still valuable: make sure the team knows what is at
stake **before** the irreversible click, and get them to the right person in
time. A team that files late has a problem; a team that published first has none
— they simply have no rights left to discuss.

## Do not overreach in the other direction

Most academic work is not patentable and should simply be published. Reflexively
warning about patents on every draft makes the warning noise, and noise gets
ignored — which means the one paper that mattered goes out unprotected. Raise it
when there is a plausible invention: a device, a process, a compound, a concrete
technical application. Not for a literature review.

And when a team has decided to publish openly rather than protect, that is a
legitimate choice made by the people whose work it is. Say the consequence once,
plainly, then help them publish.
