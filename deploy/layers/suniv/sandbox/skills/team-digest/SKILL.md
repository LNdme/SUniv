# team-digest — what changed, and what is stuck

In a small research team everyone is deep in their own piece, and the cost shows
up late: two people solving the same problem, or one person blocked for a week on
something another could have unblocked in ten minutes.

The fix is not more meetings. It is a short, honest account of what actually
moved.

## The scheduled digest

Set a cron on the project scope. Weekly suits most teams; a sprint toward a
deadline may want more.

Read the project's shared memory and `lab-notebook` entries since the last
digest, and report:

- **What moved** — the result, the decision, the section drafted — and by whom.
- **What is waiting on a person**, named. This is the highest-value line in the
  digest and the most often omitted: a blocker nobody names stays a blocker.
- **What needs a decision from the team**, rather than from one person.
- **What changed direction** — an abandoned approach, a redefined question. Half
  the team is usually still working from the old assumption.

Then stop. The temptation is to summarise everything; resist it.

## The rule that decides whether this survives

**Report only what changed.** A digest padded with restated context is skimmed
the first week, skipped the second, and filtered the third — and a digest nobody
reads is worse than none, because the team believes they are informed.

If nothing meaningful moved, say that in one line. It is true information, and it
prompts the right question.

Never manufacture activity to fill the format. An empty week is a fact about the
project, and often the most useful thing the digest can surface.

## On demand

"Where is Marc on the calibration module?" — answer from the project's shared
memory, without interrupting Marc. That is the point: the digest and the notebook
exist so people can get context without spending a colleague's attention.

Answer with what the record says and when it was recorded. Then be explicit about
its age: "as of Tuesday, from Marc's note" is useful; the same sentence without
the date invites someone to act on a stale picture. When the record is silent,
say so and suggest asking him — do not reconstruct a plausible status. A
confident guess about a teammate's progress is the same failure as an invented
citation, and it propagates faster, because people act on it immediately.

## What this is not

It is not surveillance, and the framing matters. The digest reports work — what
the team chose to record about the project — not people's activity, hours or
output rates. QM's run activity is short-lived by design and is not the source
here; the notebook is, and members control what goes into it.

If a team asks for something closer to monitoring individuals, say plainly that
this is not what the tool does, and offer the version that helps: surfacing
blockers and duplicated effort.
